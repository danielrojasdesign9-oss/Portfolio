## CONTENT

### 1. STRATEGIC FRAMING

#### Problem
Banking apps are boring. People only open them when they have to:

- **Low engagement**: Users check banking apps only for transactions, then close them
- **No motivation** for healthy financial habits (saving, budgeting, investing)
- **Financial illiteracy**: Most users don't understand basic financial concepts
- **Banking feels like a chore**: Not empowering, not engaging, not fun
- **Competition**: neobanks like Nubank are eating traditional banks' lunch

The real problem wasn't "add points." It was: **how do you make delayed gratification (saving) feel as satisfying as instant gratification (spending)?**

#### Vision
> **Banking that makes finances fun, rewarding, and educational.**

A gamified banking experience that rewards users for healthy financial behaviors — making money management feel less like a chore and more like a game you actually want to play.

### 2. METHODOLOGY — HOW I THINK

#### Research & Discovery
**Methods:** User interviews (target demo 18–35), Behavioral analysis (transaction data patterns), Competitive audit (neobanks, gamified fintech), Co-design workshops, Diary studies (banking app usage)

**Participants:** [VALIDAR — mostly 18–35 year olds]

**Duration:** [VALIDAR]

**Key Findings:**
1. **Saving is delayed gratification** — the opposite of what games excel at (immediate reward). The core design challenge.
2. **Points for everything feels like spam.** Users felt noisy and spammed by per-transaction points.
3. **Too many rewards feel patronizing.** "Here's a gold star for not going broke!" Too few feel pointless.
4. **Nobody wants to be lectured about money.** Education must be contextual, not preachy.
5. **The best gamification is invisible.** Users should feel good about their financial behavior, not about earning points.

**Artifacts:** Behavioral segmentation, Reward calibration matrix, Competitive gamification audit, Trigger moment map (where users make financial decisions)

#### Strategy & Framing
**Problem Statement (HMW):**
> How might we make healthy financial behavior feel intrinsically rewarding — so users save and learn because it feels good, not because they're chasing points?

**Design Principles:**
1. **Invisible gamification** — Celebrate the behavior, not the score
2. **Milestones, not spam** — Reward meaningful progress, not every transaction
3. **Context beats lectures** — Tips at the moment of decision
4. **Financial UX is sensitive** — Trustworthy, never gimmicky
5. **Progression over points** — Visual progress toward goals motivates

**Success Criteria:**
- Users report feeling good about their money habits, not about points
- Milestone rewards on goals (not per-transaction)
- Contextual nudges in moments of overspending
- Engagement and literacy metrics move

**Constraints & Tradeoffs:**

| Type | Description | Impact | How Addressed |
|------|-------------|--------|---------------|
| Business | Bank compliance, brand trust boundaries | High | Conservative reward language; bank approval gates |
| Technical | Integration with legacy banking systems | Medium | API layer; scoped engagement features |
| Cultural | Money is personal and sensitive | High | Trust-forward tone; user control over features |
| Product | Gamification can feel cheap/gimmicky | High | Calibrated reward balance; invisible mechanics |

#### Design & Iteration
**Exploration:** Attempt 1 was points for every transaction — failed usability, users felt spammed. Attempt 2 was milestone rewards for hitting savings goals — worked. "You saved $100 this month — here's a badge and a small reward."

**Prototyping:** Progress visualizations (goal rings, savings journeys), Badge/milestone system, Contextual tip moments, Engagement loops (daily/weekly patterns).

**Testing:** Usability tests with [VALIDAR] users; A/B on reward frequency; diary studies on emotional response to rewards.

**Key Decisions:**

| # | Context | Options | Decision | Rationale | Tradeoffs |
|---|---------|---------|----------|-----------|-----------|
| 1 | Reward model | Points per transaction / Milestone rewards / No rewards | **Milestone rewards** | Per-transaction felt like spam; milestones feel meaningful | Requires goal-setting UX |
| 2 | Reward balance | Heavy rewards / Minimal rewards / Calibrated | **Calibrated** | Too many = patronizing; too few = pointless | Ongoing calibration effort |
| 3 | Education approach | Educational screens/modules / Contextual tips / Both | **Contextual tips** | Nobody wants a lecture; tips in the decision moment work | Less "structured learning" |
| 4 | Gamification visibility | Explicit (scores, levels) / Invisible / Hybrid | **Invisible** | Users should feel good about behavior, not points | Harder to demo/show progress |

**Pivots:**
1. **Per-transaction points → Milestone rewards** — Trigger: users felt spammed, engagement didn't move. Learning: reward meaningful achievement, not activity.

#### Collaboration & Alignment
**Stakeholders:**
- Bancoomeva — Client, brand guardrails — Approver
- Banking product team — Integration, data access — Builders
- Target users (18–35) — End users — Adopters
- Compliance — Regulatory review — Gatekeeper

**Alignment Story:**
The tension was classic: banking product owners wanted visible rewards ("users need to see the points!"), while users told us (in diary studies) that heavy rewards felt patronizing. The milestone approach was the compromise that satisfied both — tangible enough to feel rewarding, infrequent enough to not feel like a participation trophy. The contextual tips came directly from user feedback: "don't lecture me, just remind me when it matters."

**Handoff:** Gamification strategy doc, Reward calibration matrix, Progress visualization specs, Contextual tip content library, Engagement loop designs.

### 5. REFLECTION

#### Learnings

| Category | Insight | Application Today |
|----------|---------|-------------------|
| Product | Gamification needs balance — too many rewards feel patronizing, too few pointless | Calibrate rewards to effort; tie to meaningful behavior |
| Product | Visual progress motivates — seeing progress toward goals increases engagement dramatically | Make progress visible, not just rewards |
| Product | Education through play — gamified learning beats traditional methods | Design learning into the flow |
| Product | Financial UX is sensitive — money is personal, must feel trustworthy not gimmicky | Conservative tone; user control over features |
| Product | Invisible gamification works best — users feel good about behavior, not points | Celebrate the behavior, hide the mechanism |
| Product | Context beats lectures — tips in the decision moment are more effective | Intervene at the moment of action |

#### What I'd Do Differently
1. **Mission moments earlier** — The "invisible gamification" insight came late. More upfront diary studies would have killed the per-transaction points idea sooner.
2. **Design the goal-setting flow first** — Milestone rewards require goals, but goal-setting UX was a bolt-on. It should have been the foundation.
3. **Segment by financial confidence** — Low-literacy users need different UX than savvy users. One-size-fits-all education missed opportunities for both.
